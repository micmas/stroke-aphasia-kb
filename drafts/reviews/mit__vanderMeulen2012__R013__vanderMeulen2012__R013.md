      Melodic Intonation Therapy: Present Controversies

      and Future Opportunities

      Ineke van der Meulen, PhD, Mieke E. van de Sandt-Koenderman, PhD, Gerard M. Ribbers, MD, PhD

      ABSTRACT. van der Meulen I, van de Sandt-Koenderman

      ME, Ribbers GM. Melodic Intonation Therapy: present contro-

      versies and future opportunities. Arch Phys Med Rehabil 2012;93

      (1 Suppl 1):S46-52.

      This article describes the state of the art of Melodic Intona-

      tion Therapy (MIT), a structured aphasia therapy program

      using the melodic aspects of language (intonation, rhythm, and

      stress) to improve language production. MIT was developed in

      the 1970s and is still used worldwide. Nevertheless, we argue

      that many questions crucial for the clinical application of MIT

      are still unanswered. First, a review of MIT effect studies is

      presented showing that evidence from well-designed group

      studies is still lacking. It is also unclear which aspects of MIT

      contribute most to its therapeutic effect and which underlying

      neural mechanisms are involved. Two cases are presented

      illustrating unsolved questions concerning MIT in clinical

      practice, such as candidacy and the best timing of this therapy.

      Key Words: Aphasia; Music therapy; Rehabilitation; Reha-

      bilitation of speech and language disorders.

      © 2012 by the American Congress of Rehabilitation

      Medicine

      F

      ROM THE EARLY DAYS of aphasia rehabilitation, many

      clinicians and researchers have been intrigued by the fact

      that patients with severe aphasia can produce well-articulated

      words while singing, but not during speech.1-4 Therefore, sing-

      ing was and is still often used in treating language and speech

      production disorders in aphasia.5-8 However, treatments based

      on melody and singing show great variety in the type of

      exercises, such as oral respiratory exercises, rhythmic syllable

      chunking, or singing familiar songs.

      The best-described aphasia therapy using melody is the

      Melodic Intonation Therapy (MIT).9-11 MIT is a hierarchically

      structured therapy, training verbal production by repeating

      melodically intoned phrases while rhythmically tapping the

      patient’s hand. Developed in the 1970s, it was one of the ﬁrst

      systematic aphasia treatment programs. It is still frequently

      used all over the world in its original form.

      In MIT, the natural prosody of the phrase, with its stress,

      rhythmic, and intonation pattern, is exaggerated and trans-

      formed into a melodic pattern of 3 or 4 tones.11,12 Crucially, the

      therapy does not involve singing familiar songs, because these

      songs are automatically generated and do not trigger the same

      processes as those underlying production of novel utterances.

      The phrases are functionally relevant in that they can be used

      in everyday life communication (eg, good morning). The

      speech-language pathologist (SLP) asks the patient to sing the

      target phrase along, while tapping the rhythm with the patient’s

      left hand. Gradually, the support from the SLP decreases until

      the patient sings the phrase independently. The ﬁnal step in-

      volves replacing singing by speaking. The target phrases in-

      crease stepwise in complexity, depending on phrase length and

      articulatory complexity.11

      The aim of this article is to discuss unanswered questions

      that are crucial for effective application of MIT in clinical

      practice. First, we review the evidence for the effectiveness of

      MIT. Further, we discuss the underlying neural processes as-

      sociated with MIT as well as the question which components of

      the MIT technique may contribute to treatment success. These

      2 issues are closely related. Originally, it was hypothesized that

      the melodic patterns used in MIT induced right hemisphere

      (RH) activation9,10,13,14 (intramodal plasticity). To date, both

      the RH hypothesis and the importance of melody are ques-

      tioned. To illustrate the relevance of these controversies for

      MIT in daily clinical practice, we present 2 cases of patients

      treated with MIT.

      LITERATURE SEARCH: STATE OF THE ART

      We searched the databases PubMed, Embase, Web-of-Sci-

      ence, PsycInfo, and CINAHL, using the search term melodic

      intonation therapy, and including all articles from the year in

      which MIT was developed (1973) until 2011. We chose this

      search term because we were speciﬁcally interested in this

      treatment program and not in melodic interventions in general.

      MIT is sometimes used in treating speech and language disor-

      ders associated with other neurologic conditions,15 or in chil-

      dren with developmental speech and language disorders.16 The

      current article focuses on patients with aphasia. All abstracts or

      complete articles were reviewed. All articles on MIT in apha-

      sia, written in English, Dutch, German, or French, and report-

      ing data on outcome measures were selected for review. Be-

      cause the majority of studies failed to report summary

      statistics, we did not impose any restrictions on the statistics.

      We carefully checked the reference lists of all relevant articles

      to identify other potentially relevant studies. Conference ab-

      stracts and posters were excluded from the present review. In

      From the Rotterdam Neurorehabilitation Research (RoNeRes), Rijndam Rehabili-

      tation Centre (van der Meulen, van de Sandt-Koenderman, Ribbers), and the Depart-

      ment of Rehabilitation Medicine, Erasmus MC (van der Meulen, van de Sandt-

      Koenderman, Ribbers), Rotterdam, the Netherlands.
